Why the average can't answer "are we losing the good ones?"

Toy numbers throughout — the shapes are the point, not the values.

Concept 1 · one average, two opposite stories

Both groups average $2,360. They are not the same group.

Each dot is one client's monthly card spend. The dashed line is the average — identical in both rows.

Toy math: 8 clients × $950 + 2 clients × $8,000 = same mean as 10 clients × $2,360.

The average is one number describing a shape it cannot see. "Leavers average $2,360" is equally true of a group we can afford to lose and a group we absolutely cannot. The mean can't tell you which one you have.

Concept 2 · the decile chart is the answer to that blindness

Line everyone up by spend, cut into 10 equal boxes, ask: where do the leavers sit?

Deciles = rank ALL clients (stayers + leavers together) by pre-unsub spend, split into tenths. Decile 1 = lowest spenders, 10 = highest. Then: what % of leavers fall in each box? If leaving had nothing to do with value, every box holds 10% — the dashed line.

This is why the pull is deciles, not another average. "How many of the good ones are we losing" is a question about the top boxes — the mean literally does not contain the answer.

Concept 3 · survivorship — how an average quietly flatters itself

Drop the clients who vanished, and the average "improves" on its own.

Five toy clients, measured then and 12 months later. One has left the bank — there's no number for them at POST, so the software skips them. Nobody decided this; it's what averaging NULLs does.

Now scale it up: leavers lost 5.9% of their tail this way, stayers only 2.6%. The group that shed more of its bottom got a bigger free boost — which is exactly why "leavers grew +25% vs stayers +24%" can't be trusted as computed. Fix: compare only clients present in both periods, and show the vanished as their own bucket instead of deleting them.

The three sentences, for the meeting

If you keep nothing else:

1 · An average describes a typical client, not a distribution — "who are we losing" is a distribution question.

2 · Deciles put the thrown-away shape back: rank by pre-unsub spend, ten boxes, see where leavers pile up.

3 · Any then-vs-now average silently drops the vanished — and our two groups vanish at different rates, so their growth rates aren't comparable until we fix the denominator.
