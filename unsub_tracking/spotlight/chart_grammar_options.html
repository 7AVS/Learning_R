Same numbers, three grammars

Card spend, Jun 2025 → Jun 2026. Stayers vs leavers. Pick the one the room can read at 4pm.

These numbers are not shippable yet. Both the spend and profit averages drop clients who stopped matching in UCP/DFP at Jun 2026 — F.avg() skips NULLs, and leavers lose 5.9% of their tail vs stayers' 2.6%. This page is about form, not the values. The shape of the argument is what you're picking.

Option A · delta bars

Show the change. Only the change.

The audience never subtracts. Two bars, one zero line, done in about a second.

WinsZero arithmetic. The one comparison that matters is the only thing on screen. Survives being glanced at.
CostsHides that leavers spend more in absolute terms ($2,360 vs $2,147). If that fact is part of your story, this chart deletes it.
Use whenThe headline is about direction, and the levels genuinely don't matter.

Option B · slopegraph, with emphasis

Show both levels and the change at once.

Leavers in colour, stayers in grey — one line is the point, the other is context.

WinsCarries the fact option A deletes — the people unsubscribing are your higher-spending cardholders. That may be the real headline.
CostsFour numbers on screen. The % labels do the subtraction for them, which fixes most of it.
Use when"Who these people are" matters as much as "what happened."

Option C · indexed to 100

Strip the levels, compare only trajectory.

Both groups start at 100. The gap at the right edge is the whole finding.

WinsThe scissor shape is unmistakable. Reads as one gesture: they split.
Costs"Indexed to 100" is a concept you have to explain, and it costs you 20 seconds you don't have. Also hides the levels, same as A.
Use whenYou have several metrics to show on one panel and need a common scale.

The rules these three are testing

  1. Never make them subtract. If the story is the change, plot the change. Levels go in the table.
  2. Emphasis beats categorical. When one series is the point, grey the other one. Two equal colours make the reader decide which matters; you should decide for them.
  3. One comparison per chart. Group × period × metric is three. Split it or drop one.
  4. The chart is layer 3, the table is layer 4. The moment a chart starts doing the table's job it stops being readable.
  5. Every axis label is a sentence they don't have to ask about. "avg monthly card spend" beats "spend".

Applies to all four sections, not just this one. Whichever option wins here becomes the grammar for the rest of the deck.
