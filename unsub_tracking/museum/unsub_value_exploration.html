1. Leaving is about age and tenure — not value

Each bar compares a segment's share of leavers against its share of stayers. Right of the line = over-represented among leavers. This is the whole finding on one chart.

over-represented among leavers under-represented 1.00 = identical to stayers
Young and new clients leave. Long-tenured and high-value clients don't. Under-25s are 1.33× over-represented among leavers; 0–2 year clients 1.25×. At the other end, 20+ year clients sit at 0.82 and the top profitability quintile at 0.84 — our most valuable clients are the least likely to go.

The question this raises for the room: a 0.66% unsub rate is not a retention problem. But losing it disproportionately among the newest clients means we're closing the email channel on the people whose relationship hasn't been built yet — before we've had the chance to build it.

2. Value is flat — which kills the "we're losing our best clients" story

Profitability quintiles, cut on the whole mailed base so "top quintile" means valuable relative to who we can actually reach. If value drove unsubscribing, this chart would slope.

Quintiles 1 through 4 sit between 1.00 and 1.06 — indistinguishable. Only the top quintile moves, and it moves down (0.84). Whatever makes someone unsubscribe, it isn't how much they're worth.

Caveat that matters: the profitability field is current-year contribution, not lifetime value — verified separately, medians rise steadily with tenure. So "low value" and "new client" are partly the same statement. Label it annual profitability anywhere this is shown.

4. Does a campaign lose single-product clients, or just mail them?

The fairness test. For each cards campaign: the share of single-product clients in who it mailed, against the share in who it lost. A gap means the campaign loses them faster than it sends to them.

share of clients mailed share of clients lost
PCQ's headline number is mostly targeting — but not entirely. 43.7% of PCQ's leavers hold one product, which looks alarming until you see PCQ mails single-product clients at 36.8%. The real signal is the +6.9pp gap: it does lose them faster than it mails them. AUH shows the same at +5.8pp. PCL and CRV are negative — they lose fewer single-product clients than they send to.

Why single-product matters: for a client who holds one product, email is the only channel we have into every product they don't hold. When they unsubscribe, that door closes on all of it at once.

5. The number nobody asked about: 427,079

Clients we emailed in Mar–May who had already unsubscribed before the window opened. Four percent of everything we sent.

These are not leavers and not stayers — they were never reachable. They sit in a third bucket precisely so they don't contaminate the comparison. But they are 6× the size of the leaver population, and every one of them is a message sent to someone who told us to stop.

Open question for the room: is this a suppression-list latency problem, a cross-program consent gap, or expected behaviour we've simply never counted? We can answer it — the data is already landed.

What I'd test next

Three things this dataset can answer without another pull.

  1. Contact volume. Do leavers get more email than stayers? We measure who they are, not how hard we hit them — and that's the most likely confound behind the age/tenure signal.
  2. Engagement. Opens and clicks are in the same vendor table. "Losing the listeners, keeping the deaf" is testable directly.
  3. The 427,079. Which programs are mailing already-opted-out clients, and how long after their unsubscribe.

Source: unsub_value_museum.py, run 2026-07-28, Mar–May 2026, bank-wide. All percentages computed on UCP-matched clients only. Rates in section 3 count clients a campaign mailed who unsubscribed via any campaign; attribution-based rates differ and are reported separately in the run output.
